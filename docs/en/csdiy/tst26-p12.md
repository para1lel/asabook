> A mathematician is a machine for turning coffee into theorems.

Let $\mathcal F\subseteq 2^{[n]}$ be a cover of $[n]$. Prove that there exists $\mathcal G\subseteq\mathcal F$ such that at least $n/H_n$ elements appear in $\mathcal G$ exactly once. Here $H_n:=\sum_{k=1}^n 1/k$ is the harmonic sum.

## Solution

This is Problem 12 of the 2026 China TST. See the [AoPS discussion thread](https://artofproblemsolving.com/community/c6h3795181p37494888).

Without loss of generality, suppose $\mathcal F=\{F_1,\ldots,F_m\}$ is a minimal cover, and let each $i\in[n]$ appear in exactly $c_i$ of its sets.  
Choose numbers $p_1+\cdots+p_m=1$ later. With probability $p_k$, choose a random $k$-element subfamily as $\mathcal G$. Then the probability that $i$ appears exactly once is
$$\sum_{k=1}^{m-c_i+1} p_k c_i\binom{m-c_i}{k-1}\Big/\binom{m}{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{m-c_i+1} p_kk \binom{m-k}{c_i-1}\Big/\binom{m}{c_i}.$$
Set $p_k:=(kH_m)^{-1}$. Then this probability is always $1/H_m$. Therefore the expected number of elements appearing exactly once in $\mathcal G$ is $n/H_m\ge n/H_n$.

## Summary

The binomial-coefficient calculation above also has a combinatorial meaning:  
the probability that a random $k$-element subset hits the first $c_i$ elements exactly once equals the probability that a random $c_i$-element subset hits the first $k$ elements exactly once. After dividing by $k$, this becomes the probability that the first element of a random $c_i$-element subset is $k$. Summing over $k$ gives total probability $1$.
